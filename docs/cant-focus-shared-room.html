Why you can't focus in a shared room —
and what actually masks a room

A flatmate on a call, a sibling in the next chair, a library that turned out not to be quiet. The problem is more specific than "noise", and the specific version has cheaper fixes.

Studying in a room with other people in it fails differently from studying alone in a distracting room. The at-home version is mostly about the room and the phone. This one is mostly about speech, and speech is a special case.

1. It is the words, not the volume

A dishwasher at the same loudness as a conversation is far easier to work through. The reason is that language gets processed whether or not you are trying: half-heard speech recruits the same machinery you are using to read, so a quiet conversation you can nearly make out is worse than a loud one you cannot. This is also why a café at a steady hum is workable and one person on a phone call three metres away is not.

The practical consequence: the goal is not silence, it is making nearby speech unintelligible. Those are different targets and they need different tools.

2. Why noise-cancelling disappoints here

Active noise cancelling is very good at low, steady sound — engines, fans, air conditioning, the rumble of a train. It is much weaker on the frequencies that carry speech, which are higher and change fast. So the headphones that made your commute silent will take the room's hum away and leave the flatmate's voice sitting on top of it, now with nothing else to hide in.

That is not a defect. It just means cancelling and masking are two jobs, and a shared room needs the second one.

4. What kind of audio actually masks speech

Three properties matter, and none of them is genre:

  • Continuous, not eventful. A track with a clear beginning, drop and end gives you something to follow. Something that just continues does not. Pads, drones and steady rain-like textures work; anything you would tap along to does less well.
  • Dark rather than bright. Energy sitting low in the spectrum covers a room without adding anything sharp of its own. There is a way to measure this, and it sorts tracks better than any label does. If the audio itself keeps catching your attention, the answer is usually a darker track, not a louder one.
  • Wordless, obviously — you cannot mask speech with speech. This rules out podcasts and, for most people, lyrics in a language you understand.

On level: masking needs the audio to sit just above the room, not far above it. The temptation is to keep raising the volume until the room disappears, which trades a distraction for hearing damage and, an hour later, a headache. If you find yourself climbing, the track is probably too bright to be doing its job.

5. The part nobody mentions: shared rooms interrupt starts, not just work

In a room by yourself, an interruption costs you the thread. In a shared room, most of what you lose happens before you start: you sit down, someone says something, you answer, you check your phone, and twenty minutes later you have not opened the file. The work never got interrupted because it never began.

Which is why the useful move is to make starting take one action instead of several — decide the night before what the block is for, have the sound and length already chosen, and start the timer before the conversation can happen. A block that is already running is much easier to defend than one you are about to start.
