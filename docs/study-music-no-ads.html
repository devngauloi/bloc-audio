Study music with no ads —
what "no ads" actually means

Three different claims wear the same two words. Here is how to check each one yourself, without taking anyone's word for it — including ours.

Search for study music without ads and every result says the same thing on the store page. Install four of them and you will find they meant four different things. The phrase is doing at least three separate jobs:

What it can mean What still happens
1. No banner or video ads in the app The usual claim. Says nothing about the other two.
2. No ads inside the audio Free playlist services insert spots between tracks. Silent for twenty minutes, then a voice at minute twenty-one, in the middle of your session.
3. No trackers An app with no visible ads can still ship an advertising SDK that reports what you do. You are not being shown ads here — you are being used to aim them elsewhere.

Most people check number one, because it is the only one you can see. Numbers two and three are where the surprises live, and both are testable in about a minute.

How to test each one, yourself

  • For "no ads in the app": use it on the free tier for a full session without touching anything. Some apps show nothing for the first few minutes by design.
  • For "no ads in the audio": put the phone in airplane mode and press play. If the music stops, the audio was being streamed and anything can be inserted into that stream later. If it keeps playing, the audio is on your device.
  • For "no trackers": the app is hard to inspect without tools, but its website is not. Open a terminal and run curl -s on their homepage, then read what it loads. Fonts from a font CDN, an analytics script, a chat widget, a Google-hosted store badge — each of those is a third party being told you visited. A site that claims privacy while pulling six external files is telling you something about how the company thinks, and it costs you one command to find out.

That last test is worth running on us too. This page loads its stylesheet, its icons and its preview image from this domain and nothing else — zero third-party requests, by rule and not by accident. If that ever stops being true, the test will say so before we do.

The question almost nobody asks a music library

Here is one you will not see in a review: how many distinct recordings does that library actually contain?

Not how many tracks are listed. How many are genuinely different pieces of music. Those two numbers come apart more often than you would expect, because a library assembled from generated or licensed material accumulates the same recording under several names — a raw export, a normalised version, a re-encode with a different filename.

We know this because it happened to us, and the only reason we found it is that we went looking. In August 2026 we hashed every row in the catalogue — 152 at the time — and compared them byte for byte. The result:

  • Seven pairs were byte-identical. Fourteen listings, seven pieces of music. Plus a third copy of one track that had been re-encoded, so its bytes differed while the music did not.
  • Eight rows came out of the catalogue that day.

Then the interesting part. Refilling those eight slots, the picker screened every candidate three ways: hash against all live files, spectral centroid against the mood's measured range, and duration. Eight candidates passed all three. Two of them were still the same recording as a track already in the library — caught only by correlating the actual waveforms, at r = 0.99998 and r = 0.99999.

The lesson generalises past us: a hash proves two files are not byte-identical and nothing more. A re-encoded copy of a track already in the library sails through a hash check, matches its own mood's acoustic profile perfectly, and has exactly the right duration. Every cheap filter says it is a new song. It is not.

So when a library advertises hundreds of tracks, the honest question is whether anyone ever measured. We can tell you what our number means because we built the check. We cannot tell you what anyone else's number means, and neither can they unless they did the same work.
