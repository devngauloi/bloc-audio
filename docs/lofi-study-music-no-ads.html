Lofi study music with no ads —
what makes a track work, and what makes one fail

"Lofi" describes a production style, not an effect on your attention. Two tracks can both be lofi and only one of them lets you keep reading. The difference is measurable, and once you know what to measure you stop guessing.

Most advice about study music stops at instrumental, nothing with words. That rules out the obvious failure and leaves the interesting one untouched: plenty of wordless lofi still pulls your eyes off the page. Having built and measured a library of instrumental tracks, here is what actually separates them.

1. Brightness, and why it is the number that matters

There is a single measurement that predicts this better than genre labels do: spectral centroid. It is the frequency the energy of a track balances around — the centre of gravity of its sound. Low centroid reads as warm and dim; high centroid reads as present and forward.

Sorting an instrumental library by that number gives an ordering that lines up with how the tracks behave in a session:

Character Relative centroid What it does in a session
Dark, hushed pieces lowest Nearly disappears. Best when the work is hard and the room is quiet.
Soft pads and drones low Fills silence without adding events to track.
Warm lofi and chillhop middle The classic study sound. Enough motion to mask a room, not enough to follow.
Bright, jazzy, guitar-forward highest Good for admin, email, tidying notes. It asks to be listened to.

The practical version: if you keep noticing the music, you are probably a step too bright, not a step too loud. Moving down one row usually fixes it, and moving down is not the same as turning the volume down.

2. Piano is a dark instrument, which surprises everyone

This is worth stating on its own because the intuition runs the other way. Solo felt piano — the sound people picture when they say "calm study music" — measures near the bottom of the range, alongside drones. Meanwhile the tracks that measure at the top are chillhop, jazzhop and guitar.

We learned this the expensive way. One of the four channels in our app was labelled piano + strings for months. When the library was finally measured, the one solo piano source landed in a completely different channel from the one carrying that label, and everything that did measure into it was jazz and guitar. The label was true as text and false about the audio behind it — and no test we had could catch that, because a label is not a claim a computer can check. It now says bright, jazzy, which is what it always was.

Why this matters when you are choosing a playlist: a description written by whoever made it is a description of their intention. The sound is a separate question.

3. Loudness decides whether an hour is tiring

Music mastered for a playlist is mastered to compete — loud, compressed, forward. That is correct for a bus and wrong for a desk, because the thing that tires you over an hour is not volume, it is that everything stays at the same insistent level with no room in it.

Two adjustments do most of the work, and you can hear both:

  • A consistent, low target level. Our library is mastered to a single integrated loudness across every track, so nothing jumps when the next one starts. A playlist assembled from different sources will not do this, and the jumps are what make you reach for the volume mid-session — which is itself an interruption.
  • Less low end than a music mix wants. Sub-bass is fine in headphones on a walk and fatiguing at a desk over ninety minutes. Rolling off what sits below the musical range, and easing the weight under roughly 250 Hz, makes the same track sit further back without making it quieter.

You can test this on any playlist in about a minute: play a track at your usual level, then skip to the next three. If you adjust the volume even once, the set is not mastered to a common target, and every one of those adjustments is a moment you were not working.

4. How long the pool needs to be

The number that matters is not how many tracks exist, it is how many minutes play before you hear one twice. Music that continues through breaks means a 25/5 session is about 30 continuous minutes, a 50/10 is 60, and a 90/15 is 105. A pool shorter than the session repeats inside it, and a repeat is the most attention-grabbing event a background track can produce — you notice it exactly because you have heard it before.

So the honest question to ask any study music app is not how many songs. It is how many minutes in the mood I actually use.
